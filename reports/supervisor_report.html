Summary

  1. The test bed reproduces the study. Run at each virtual crack's true depth, the study's own engine gives the same U90 reductions as the frozen predictions at all 5 study depths (plate), within 1.6 points. Filters whose model is right give honest 90 % intervals (89–94 %). Any gap between prediction and result below is therefore about the sensor and the estimator, not about the simulation.
  2. Approach 1's static claims hold, with smaller margins. One sizing at sd 0.17 mm gives 74 % of the exact-depth benefit (study 86 %). Ten sizings at 0.1 mm cut the spread by 31 % against one (study 30–40 %), and at 0.5 mm by 14 % (study about 10 %): repetition pays even with a coarse sensor.
  3. Learning the growth rate is worth more than knowing depth, but the simple filter overstates its confidence. Followed from below the detection limit and monitored continuously at sd 0.2 mm, a filter that learns one growth-rate multiplier removes 83 % of the plate's spread at 1 mm, close to the 81 % of an oracle that knows depth, shape and λ, and far beyond the 31 % for depth alone. Its intervals then cover only 85 % instead of 90 % (70 % after more than 30 readings at sd 0.1 mm), because NASGRO's growth curve differs between cracks in shape, not only in level. A filter that carries the full NASGRO uncertainty stays honest. Approach 1 needs that kind of filter, or a model-discrepancy term.
  4. Approach 3: continuous monitoring buys back service life, not safety. With cracks starting below detection, every periodic scheme failed 0 % of 120 components, as did continuous monitoring within noise (0.8 %). The difference is life given up by acting early: 1.06 nominal lives for current practice every 20 % of life, 0.40 for the learning filter every 10 %, 0.02 continuous. Two risks dominate: a silently de-bonded array (84 % failed) and exact readings fed to the overconfident filter (6.7 % failed).
  5. Every sensor, cost and gate value is a PLACEHOLDER chosen before the runs. Specimen counts are reduced to fit four cores. The conclusions above are stated so they can be re-read once the inputs are agreed (section 8).

1 · What was built

The sensitivity study answers one question: how much of the spread in predicted life disappears if an input is known exactly? A real inspection never knows an input exactly. It measures crack depth with an error, at certain times, and something has to turn those readings into a forecast. The test bed asks the second question: how much of the spread disappears when a real ultrasonic sensor and a real estimator learn it? The ratio of the two is each approach's realised fraction.

Each crack grows by the validated NASGRO pipeline, with every NASGRO input drawn from the study's industrial spreads. The pipeline is pinned by commit and hash-checked
2 · Sensor

No reading, one sizing, periodic, continuous; current practice, a full-NASGRO Bayesian filter, a one-parameter growth-rate filter, Bayes factors
4 · Score
Same metric as the study

Every arm sees the same virtual specimens and, where two arms take a reading at the same cycle, the same measurement error (common random numbers). Differences between arms are therefore differences between methods, not between random draws.

A fraction of 1 means the sensor and estimator deliver everything the study promised; above 1 means they deliver more, because a record of readings carries information the study's static view leaves out. A narrow forecast only counts if it is honest, so every result is read together with its coverage: the share of true lives that fall inside the nominal 90 % interval. Coverage well below 90 % means the forecast is overconfident.

2 · Why the test bed can be trusted

Before any result about ultrasound can be read, three things have to hold: the virtual cracks must grow exactly as in the study, the perfect-information arm must reproduce the study's numbers, and the estimators' intervals must be honest when their model is right. Each is checked directly.

Two ways of choosing when to forecast

The study defines U90 at fixed crack depths (0.2–4 mm). The simulation therefore scores each forecast at the moment the true crack reaches those depths, which is the like-for-like comparison of spread. That moment depends on the truth, though, so it is not a fair test of calibration: a crack that reaches 1 mm early is, by selection, a fast one. Coverage is therefore measured at moments that barely depend on how fast a crack grows: random and fixed fractions of each crack's life (where the well-specified toy filter covers 89–92 %, as it should) and a fixed cycle count (check B), which does not depend on the crack at all.

3 · Approach 1: learn each component's growth rate from repeated sizing

The study's claims for this approach, and what the simulation found when a sensor and a filter had to earn them.

3.1 One sizing: how precise must it be?

Prediction (P1.2). Sizing to a third of today's ultrasonic error (sd 0.17 mm instead of 0.5 mm) captures 86 % of the benefit of knowing depth exactly at 1 mm (plate).

Simulation. Current practice, given one fresh sizing when the true crack reaches 1 mm, captures 74 % at sd 0.17 mm and 68 % at the study's 0.2 mm target. Even at 0.05 mm it reaches 85 %, not 100 %.

3.2 Repeated sizing: does repetition pay only with precision?

Prediction (P1.4, check B). Ten sizings at 0.1 mm over the first 20 % of life cut the forecast spread by 40 % against one sizing; at 0.5 mm repeating adds about 10 %.

Simulation. The same design on the rebuilt NASGRO model, with the well-specified filter (as in check B): 31 % at 0.1 mm, 17 % at 0.2 mm and 14 % at 0.5 mm. The practical growth-rate filter gives almost the same cuts (30 %, 17 %, 15 %). Coverage of every arm in this test is 89 %–94 %.

On the bar the cuts are 21 % (0.1 mm), 11 % (0.2 mm) and 17 % (0.5 mm), against check B's 31 %, 15 % and 10 %; coverage 88 %–93 %.

Reading 3.2

The direction of check B holds: repeated sizing always sharpens the forecast, and by most at 0.1 mm on the plate. Two details differ. At 0.1 mm the cut is at the bottom of the predicted 30–40 % on the plate (31 %) and below it on the bar. At 0.5 mm repetition is worth more than the predicted 10 % (14 % plate, 17 % bar), because averaging several coarse readings recovers some of the depth precision a single coarse reading lacks. “Precision and repetition only pay together” is therefore too strong: repetition pays on its own, and precision makes it pay more.

3.3 Learning the growth rate: how much of the λ group can a sensor recover?

Prediction (P1.3, check A). With the crack state exact, the inputs that act as one growth-rate multiplier λ (stress, the material fit, closure through α) hold 62–84 % of the original spread. Known exactly, they take the plate's U90 at 1 mm from 1.24 (study baseline) to 0.23 decades in the simulation's oracle, a 81 % reduction, inside the predicted range.

Simulation. Cracks were followed from 0.15 mm, below the detection limit, and forecast when they reached 1 mm. With sizing every 10 % of life at sd 0.2 mm, the growth-rate filter's U90 is 0.88, already as sharp as knowing depth exactly (0.86). With continuous monitoring it is 0.21 at sd 0.2 mm and 0.12 at 0.1 mm, level with the oracle that knows depth, shape and λ.

3.4 But is the sharp forecast honest?

Sharpness alone is not the goal. The growth-rate filter assumes the whole difference between cracks is one multiplier on C. In NASGRO it is not: the threshold, the exponent n and toughness change the shape of the growth curve, not only its level. With a few readings that model error is hidden by measurement noise. With many precise readings the filter becomes certain of the wrong curve. The machinery itself is not at fault: on the toy problem, where one multiplier is the true model, the same filter with continuous monitoring covers 92 % and 90 %. Its coverage falls from 93 % with no readings to 70 % with more than 30 readings at sd 0.1 mm. The full-NASGRO filter, which carries the shape uncertainty, stays at 86 %–92 % up to ten readings. Beyond that it also slips (79 %–86 % with 11–30 readings, with wide intervals), because its 4,000 weighted samples thin out as the data sharpen; that is a sampling limit with a known cure (more samples or a resample-move step [15]), not a model error.

4 · Approach 3: inspection intervals, periodic against continuous

The study makes no interval prediction. It does say that below about 1 mm the undetected crack state leads the spread (plate 0.2 mm 11 %, 0.5 mm 19 %; P3.1). The simulation therefore asks the decision question directly: with the same sensor, how many components fail, and how much life is thrown away, under each inspection scheme?

Every arm uses one rule, after Straub and Faber [10]: act (repair or replace) as soon as the forecast probability of failure before the next planned reading, plus a repair lead time of 1 % of life, reaches pact = 1 % (0.1 % and 5 % are in the run files and change the picture little). Probability of detection follows MIL-HDBK-1823A [9] with a50 0.15 mm and a90 0.30 mm (PLACEHOLDER). Costs are in units of one repair; a nominal life is the life of a nominal crack from its start depth.

4.1 Cracks starting near 0.2 mm, below reliable detection

Every scheme that inspects periodically with the realistic sensor failed 0 % of 120 components. They differ in how early they act. Current practice every 20 % of life gives up 1.06 nominal lives on average, because without a growth rate it must assume the worst between visits. The learning filter at the same spacing gives up 0.87, at 10 % spacing 0.40, and with continuous monitoring 0.02, with 0.8 % failed.

Two results the study could not have shown

Perfect readings with an imperfect model fail components. Perfect inspection with the growth-rate filter failed 6.7 % (continuous) and 5.0 % (every 10 %), more than the realistic sensor. This is section 3.4's overconfidence in practice: exact readings make the one-parameter filter certain of the wrong curve, and it acts too late. Perfect information, which knows the true growth rate, failed none.

A silent sensor failure dominates the risk. When the installed array de-bonds part-way through life (median 40 % of life, PLACEHOLDER after An et al. [19]) and nothing notices, 84 % of components fail: the rule only re-forecasts when a reading arrives, so the last forecast stands. This is the worst case, and a cheap sensor-health check removes it. A sensor that undersizes by 0.1 mm without the filter knowing changed almost nothing (0.0 % failed).

4.2 What continuous monitoring may cost

The break-even cost is the most a continuous system may cost per nominal life, in repairs, for it to match periodic inspection with the same sensor and filter (periodic readings at 0.05 repairs each; life given up valued at 2 repairs per nominal life; all PLACEHOLDERS). At a failure cost of 200 repairs, one failure in 120 under continuous monitoring outweighs its savings, which is within the statistical noise of 120 specimens.

5 · Approach 2: residual stress

The study found that whether a residual stress is present, and its sign, matters far more than its size (P2.1, P2.2). The simulation asks whether the depth record alone, or an LCR ultrasonic survey of the stress, can tell which of three cases a component is in: tensile as-welded, none, or compressive (peened).

7 · What the results do not show

  • One model family. Truth and filters are both NASGRO with Newman closure. The “model wrong” variant adds closure and stress-intensity errors and a wandering growth rate, but real cracks can differ in ways no variant here contains (roughness- and oxide-induced closure, crack coalescence, changing shape). Laboratory growth records with independent ultrasonic readings, such as An et al. [19], are the next test.
  • PLACEHOLDER inputs. Sizing error and its correlation, POD, LCR error, the decision threshold, and every cost are placeholders chosen before the runs (docs/assumptions.md). Approach 1 is shown across sizing errors so the conclusion can be read at any agreed value; Approach 3's costs are bracketed. Peng et al. [7] and Felice et al. [8] should set the sizing error, An et al. [19] the installed-array behaviour.
  • Specimen counts. 100–400 virtual specimens per variant, limited by four cores. Every interval shown is a 95 % interval over specimens (Wilson for failure rates [16], bootstrap over specimens for coverage [17]); differences inside those intervals are not findings.
  • Checks A and B. The scoping page's checks A and B came from the original pipeline, rerun with κ and the SIF error fixed, and that rerun is not committed. The simulation recomputes them on the rebuild, so they are compared as cuts and shares, not absolute U90.
  • Not yet built. Approach 4 (crack opening, a change of stress ratio) and Approach 5 (scattering geometry and tilt) need an opening sensor and an angle sensor and are the second round. Approach 6 (joint model) has no prediction to test.
  • Correction made during this work. The first runs showed that current practice was penalised for readings just below 1 mm, because the study's case code switches to its undetected-crack model (tilt sd 15° instead of 5°) below 1 mm. A sized crack is detected, so the current-practice arm now says so; a regression test pins it, and every run affected was repeated (commit 57a031c).

8 · Next steps

  1. Agree the PLACEHOLDER inputs (sizing error and its correlation, POD, LCR error, decision threshold, costs, gates), then rerun every variant at the configured specimen counts. The code, configurations and seeds are fixed, so this is a compute job (about 260 CPU-hours; README).
  2. Approach 1: make the learning filter honest. Add a model-discrepancy term to the growth-rate filter (Kennedy and O'Hagan [20]), or run the full-NASGRO filter with more samples, and show coverage holds under continuous monitoring. Then test both on real growth records: replicate-specimen data such as Virkler et al. [22] for the rate scatter, and An et al.'s installed-array records [19] for real readings.
  3. Approach 3: price the de-bonding risk. A sensor-health check (does the array still see its back wall?) is cheap and turns a silent loss of data into a known gap, which the decision rule can then cover with a manual inspection.
  4. Approach 2: choose between the depth record and an LCR survey once the LCR error achievable on the target components is known; the table in 5.3 gives the error needed.
  5. Second round: Approach 4 (crack opening under a change of stress ratio, E-04) and Approach 5 (scattering geometry and tilt, E-05).
